An Argument in Three Acts

I.

And

There are hundreds of cocktail recipes in this book, and a small army of ingredients — fruits and syrups, spirits and bitters — all jostling for room on the page.

II.

But

The moment you commit to a base spirit — or even to a single supporting note — the field of possibilities contracts. Most ingredients cannot coexist with what you have chosen.

III.

Therefore

Cocktails are not random mixtures. They follow a hidden grammar of flavor, structure, and tradition. To pick one ingredient is to commit to a whole branch of the family tree.

What this visualization teaches us

Cocktails look chaotic on a shelf — gin beside crème de cacao beside grenadine — but they are governed by an unwritten grammar. A handful of spirits act as subjects; a small vocabulary of citrus, sweetness, and bitters provide predicates; and the rest are decorations on a sentence that already makes sense. Move through this book by selecting an ingredient and you will see that grammar enforce itself in real time: dozens of ingredients dim because they have never, in any recipe, sat beside the ones you chose. The act of interaction is what reveals the structure — a static list of 989 cocktails hides it. By forcing the page to react, we expose pairings that bartenders absorbed through repetition, and we make the implicit knowledge of the trade visible to anyone holding a glass.

Prototype Writeup

What have we done so far?

So far, we have built a working interactive prototype for Cocktail Flavor Atlas: An Interactive Recipe Grimoire. We loaded and cleaned the TidyTuesday Boston Cocktails dataset, grouped the data into real cocktail recipes, and created a filtering system based on base spirits and ingredients. Users can choose a base spirit such as gin, rum, vodka, tequila, whiskey, brandy, or mezcal, and the page updates to show only real cocktails from the dataset that match that choice. We also implemented ingredient buttons that remain clickable only when they can still lead to at least one real cocktail, while impossible combinations become disabled. In addition, the prototype includes a D3 ingredient frequency bar chart and cocktail recipe cards that update as the user explores the recipe space. The visual design currently follows an ancient cocktail grimoire theme, using parchment, old ink, tavern-inspired colors, and recipe-book styling to support the project's story.

What will be the most challenging part to design, and why?

The most challenging part of our project will be designing interactions that feel meaningful rather than decorative. Our goal is not just to let users filter cocktails, but to help them understand the hidden structure behind classic drink recipes. This means we need to make the disabled ingredient states, ingredient frequency chart, and recipe cards clearly explain how the possible cocktail space narrows after each choice. Another challenge will be balancing the grimoire visual style with readability and data clarity, because an old parchment design can easily become too decorative or hard to read. We also need to decide whether to add a deeper visualization, such as an ingredient co-occurrence network or cocktail similarity map, without making the page confusing. For the final version, the design should feel like an immersive recipe book while still functioning as a clear explorable explanation.
